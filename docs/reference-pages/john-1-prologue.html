Who preserves which verse

Each row is a manuscript, each column a verse. A filled square means at least part of that verse survives in that manuscript. John’s opening chapter is unusually well covered: the two great early papyri both begin at 1:1, and so do nearly all the complete Gospel books.

Where the manuscripts disagree

Most differences in these eighteen verses are spelling and word order. Four places matter for meaning. The printed reading of the modern critical edition (Nestle–Aland, 28th edition) is outlined.

Saved for the translation sweep

John 1:1c · translation question, not a manuscript variant

“The Word was God”, “a god”, or “divine”?

καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγοςRead the same way in every early Greek manuscript that preserves the verse.

The manuscripts agree; the debate is grammatical. Here θεός has no article and stands before the verb. Colwell (1933) observed that definite predicate nouns placed before the verb usually lack the article, which leaves room for “God”. Harner (1973) argued that such nouns are chiefly qualitative, stressing what the Word is, which is why some translations use “divine” or “what God was”. “A god” is the reading of a minority of translations. This will be treated in full in the translation sweep.

Scholarly works
  • Colwell, E. C. “A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament.” Journal of Biblical Literature 52 (1933): 12–21.
  • Harner, Philip B. “Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1.” Journal of Biblical Literature 92 (1973): 75–87.

The Latin layer

Latin translations were made from Greek manuscripts older than most that survive, so they can preserve early readings, like the singular at 1:13. These are key Latin witnesses up to AD 900; verse-level coverage for the Old Latin manuscripts is still to be mapped.
