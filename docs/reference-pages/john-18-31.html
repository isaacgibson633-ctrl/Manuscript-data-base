How these manuscripts got their dates

Almost none of the manuscripts above say when they were made. Each date on this page is a scholarly argument built from several kinds of evidence, and the arguments are only as strong as the evidence each manuscript offers. These are the kinds of evidence, roughly from most used to least.

Handwriting

Palaeographers compare a manuscript’s script with documents that state their own date (contracts, receipts, petitions) and with the few dated books. They look at the shapes of telltale letters (alpha, mu, epsilon, omega), whether letters fit a square or a narrow frame, the contrast between thick and thin strokes that comes from the angle of the pen’s nib, small hooks or serifs at stroke ends, and how evenly letters sit between two imaginary lines.

Book construction

Roll or codex, page size, how many columns, how the gatherings of leaves (quires) were built and ruled. Sinaiticus has four columns a page and Vaticanus three, while most later Gospel books have two or one. The study of these features is called codicology.

Reading aids

Features invented at known times give a ‘not before’ date. Eusebius of Caesarea devised his Gospel section numbers and canon tables in the early 4th century, so a first-hand copy of them must be later. Accents and breathing marks only become routine centuries later.

Dated anchors

A few books carry a scribe’s dated note (a colophon). Vat. gr. 1666 is a majuscule dated 800; the Uspensky Gospels (minuscule 461) are dated 835, the earliest dated Greek minuscule; 028 S is dated 949. These fixed points calibrate everything around them.

Where it was found

A manuscript excavated alongside dated documents, like 𝔓60 at Nessana, has a context to test its date against. Most early papyri, including 𝔓52 and 𝔓66, were bought from dealers, so that evidence is lost.

Carbon dating and AI

Radiocarbon dating has rarely been used on New Testament manuscripts: it damages the object and often returns ranges as wide as handwriting does. Recent work combines it with machine analysis of handwriting, so far tested on the Dead Sea Scrolls (Popović et al. 2025).

A field guide to the scripts

Script styles are families, not timestamps: a style could be used for generations, and scribes working at the same time wrote differently. The ranges below are broad on purpose.

1st–3rd c.

Early literary hands

Rounded capitals, sometimes with small decorative hooks; the related ‘severe style’ mixes narrow and broad letters with a slight slope. This is 𝔓52 and 𝔓66 territory, and it is where dating is least precise.

2nd/3rd–9th c.

Biblical majuscule

Defined by Guglielmo Cavallo (1967). Each letter fills a square module, and round letters are true circles. The 4th-century ‘canonical’ form has little thick–thin contrast and no ornament (01, 03). Later it gains heavy contrast, blobbed stroke ends and enlarged initials (02, 04), and it lingers in a stiff late form through the 9th century.

5th–8th c.

Alexandrian majuscule

Rounded and supple, with circular epsilon, omicron and sigma. Common in Egypt, and for Coptic as well as Greek.

6th–10th c.

Ogival (‘pointed’) majuscule

Narrow, oval letters with strong contrast, written upright or sloping. Used for many late Gospel books after most other writing had moved to minuscule.

9th c. onward

Minuscule

A faster, joined lowercase script that takes over Greek book production from about 800. Majuscule survives for display and liturgical books, which is why this page still has so many 9th-century majuscules.

Why dates get disputed

Comparing unlike things

Early papyri of books are compared with dated documents, but book hands and everyday hands developed differently. Barker (2011) proposes first placing a manuscript in its ‘graphic stream’ and comparing it only with dated examples from that stream.

Styles outlive fashions

A trained scribe could keep writing an old style for decades, and a style could be revived. A difference in hand does not always mean a difference in date.

Tiny samples

𝔓52 preserves around a hundred letters on its two sides. The fewer letters survive, the fewer features there are to compare, and the wider an honest date range has to be.

What is at stake

An early 𝔓52 was long used to argue that John was written before about AD 100. Orsini and Clarysse (2012) warn against ‘theological palaeography’, the pull toward early dates for Christian texts; Porter (2013) argues that the sceptics have over-corrected.

How this list was made

Numbers

Manuscripts are named by their Gregory–Aland number, the standard catalogue kept by the Institute for New Testament Textual Research (INTF) in Münster. 𝔓 marks a papyrus; numbers starting with 0 are parchment majuscules (capital-letter manuscripts).

Dates

The headline date is the INTF catalogue date. Where scholars have argued for other dates, the entry gives the range and names who proposed what. Almost all of these dates come from handwriting style (palaeography), not from carbon dating or a dated colophon.

Confidence labels

Firm: specialists broadly agree within about a century. Debated: published proposals differ by more than a century, or the catalogue date has been seriously challenged. The label is about the date, not the manuscript’s importance.

What “contains the verse” means

At least part of John 18:31 survives on the page. For fragments, that can mean a handful of letters. Contents come from published catalogue descriptions and each manuscript’s recorded gaps (lacunae).

Photographs

Images stay with the libraries and projects that made them, so every entry links out rather than copying pictures. The INTF Virtual Manuscript Room link works for every entry; other links go to the holding library or to CSNTM where a viewer exists.

Neutral language

Entries describe what a manuscript is, when it was likely made and how it reached its current home. They don’t argue for or against the reliability of the Gospels.
