John 2: the wedding at Cana and the temple

Three scenes: water turned into wine at a wedding in Cana, Jesus driving the traders out of the Jerusalem temple, and a short note on the signs he did at Passover. For each verse: which Greek manuscripts up to AD 900 preserve it, where they disagree, and which Latin manuscripts up to 900 carry it.

The passage, verse by verse

A literal translation of our own, following the modern critical Greek text. Greek is shown where the manuscripts differ. Blue tags mark a manuscript variant, amber tags a translation question saved for a later sweep; the count is how many Greek witnesses up to 900 preserve the verse.

Who preserves which verse

Two of the great early books drop out here: Codex Ephraemi has lost John 1:41–3:32, and Codex Bezae’s Greek has a gap from 1:17 to the end of chapter 3. In their place come small but early witnesses: 0162, a 4th-century parchment leaf from Oxyrhynchus with 2:11–22, and a Greek–Coptic leaf (0127) from the White Monastery in Egypt.

Where the manuscripts disagree

John 2 is textually quiet: the NET Bible’s notes flag a single variant for the whole chapter. Three places are worth a reader’s attention. The reading printed in the modern critical edition (Nestle–Aland, 28th edition) is outlined.

Saved for the translation sweep

John 2:4 · translation question, not a manuscript variant

“Woman, what is that to me and to you?”

τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί, γύναι;Read the same way in the early Greek manuscripts.

A Semitic idiom (found also in the Greek Old Testament, for example Judges 11:12 and 2 Kings 3:13) whose tone is hard to carry into English: renderings range from “what concern is that to you and to me?” to “why do you involve me?”. The address “woman” was not rude in Greek, though it sounds so in modern English. Both belong to the translation sweep.

John 2:6 · translation question, not a manuscript variant

How big were the jars?

ἀνὰ μετρητὰς δύο ἢ τρεῖςRead the same way in the early Greek manuscripts.

A metrētēs was a liquid measure of roughly 39 litres (about 10 US gallons), so each jar held something like 80 to 120 litres. Translations variously keep the ancient unit, convert it, or round it; the sweep will set one policy for ancient measures across the site.

The Latin layer

From this chapter on, the Latin list follows the manuscript catalogue of the Vetus Latina Iohannes edition, which covers every Old Latin manuscript of John; early Vulgate books are added below it. The Latin matters most at 2:3, where several Old Latin manuscripts share Sinaiticus’s longer text.
